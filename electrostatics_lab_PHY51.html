Concept

Types of Charge & Coulomb's Law

There are exactly two kinds of electric charge: positive (+) and negative (−). The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C).

The space around a charge is an electric field — a region where another charge feels a force. The arrows on the field lines point the way a positive charge would be pushed.

AR Mode Use your camera: charge A follows your right index finger, B your left. Point at the A / B buttons on screen to flip a sign.

Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Change the signs, separation and size of the charges below and watch the field lines, the force reading and the charges' own motion respond. A positive charge is drawn as a red sphere and a negative charge as a blue sphere; either sphere grows or shrinks with the charge magnitude (q).

Every atom is built from three particles: protons (positive), neutrons (neutral — no charge at all), and electrons (negative). Protons and neutrons are bound tightly together in a tiny, dense nucleus at the centre; electrons occupy the much larger space around it.

proton (+) neutron (0) electron (−)

Most everyday objects are electrically neutral because they contain an equal number of protons and electrons, so the positive and negative charges exactly cancel out. Charging an object never creates or destroys charge — it just unbalances this equal count by moving electrons in or out.

Charging a Body

Charging an object means giving it an overall (net) charge. This always happens by the addition or removal of electrons — protons stay locked inside atomic nuclei and are never transferred between objects.

Rub the rod against the cloth: electrons hop from one surface to the other. Whichever object gains electrons ends up negative; whichever loses electrons ends up positive — by exactly equal amounts, since charge is conserved.

Electric Fields

An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge placed there.

Point charge: lines radiate out from +, and in towards −. Charged conducting sphere: the same radial pattern outside; the charge sits on the surface. Parallel plates: straight, equally spaced lines from + to − (a uniform field; end effects ignored).

Release the test charge: a + charge moves along the field; a − charge moves against it.

Conductors & Insulators

Experiment: charged sphere A touches one end of the rod. Press Connect to bring neutral sphere B up to the other end. If B gains charge, the material is a conductor; if not, it is an insulator.

Electron model: in a conductor (e.g. copper, iron) some electrons are free to move through the lattice of fixed positive ions, so charge can flow. In an insulator (plastic, glass, wood) all electrons are bound to their atoms and only vibrate, so charge cannot flow.

Producing & Detecting Charge

Production: rubbing two different insulating materials together (friction) transfers electrons between them, charging both — this is how the test rod is charged.

Detection: the gold-leaf electroscope detects charge. Charge spreads down the stem to two thin leaves, which — carrying the same sign — repel and diverge. The amount of divergence shows roughly how much charge is present, and comparing an approaching rod's effect on an already-charged electroscope reveals its sign.
