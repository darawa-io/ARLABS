Concept

Reading Rulers & Measuring Cylinders

A ruler measures length. Line up the zero mark exactly with one end of the object, look straight down at the scale (avoid parallax error), and read where the other end lines up — estimating to half of the smallest division.

A measuring cylinder measures volume. Liquid surfaces curve into a meniscus; for water and most liquids, read the scale at eye level at the bottom of the curve.

Adjust the block and the liquid level below, then try the practice reading — type in what you measure and check your answer.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Density is defined as mass per unit volume:

ρ = m / V

where ρ is density (g/cm³ or kg/m³), m is mass (measured with a balance), and V is volume.

For a regular solid like this block, volume is found from its measured dimensions: V = L × W × H. For a liquid, volume is simply read straight off a measuring cylinder.

Pick a material, resize the block, and watch V, m and ρ update. Then try to identify a hidden material purely from its density.

Irregular Solids: The Displacement Method

An irregularly shaped solid that sinks has no simple length × width × height. Instead, its volume is found by displacement:

1. Record the water volume, V₁.
2. Lower the object in completely — it pushes water up to a new level, V₂.
3. The object's volume is the difference:

Vobject = V₂ − V₁

Once you know the volume and the mass (from a balance), density follows from ρ = m / V, exactly as before.

Floating & Sinking

Whether an object floats or sinks in a liquid depends only on comparing two densities:

• If the object's density is less than the liquid's density, it floats.
• If it's greater than the liquid's density, it sinks.
• If the two are equal, it hovers, neither rising nor sinking.

A floating object sinks in only far enough that the fraction submerged equals the ratio of the two densities: %submerged = ρobject / ρliquid × 100.

Layering Immiscible Liquids

When liquids that don't mix (are immiscible) are poured into the same container, they don't stay in the order you poured them. They settle into layers ordered purely by density: the densest liquid ends up at the bottom, the least dense at the top.

This works the same way as floating and sinking — each liquid layer "floats" on anything denser beneath it.

Pour up to four liquids in any order you like, then watch them settle — and check whether your predicted stacking order matches.
