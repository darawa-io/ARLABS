Concept

Electric Field & Force on a Test Charge

A test charge is a small positively charged object of negligible size and mass, used only to probe the field — it doesn't disturb it.

E = F / q

An electric field exists at a point if a charge placed there would feel a force. Its direction is the direction that test charge would move.

E is measured in N/C. The force on any charge q sitting in a field is F = qE

Toggle the test charge on to see F appear — same direction as E if q₀ is positive, opposite if negative.

Field of a Single Point Charge

Combining E = F/q with Coulomb's law gives the field of one point charge:

E = (1/4πε₀) · Q/r² = kQ/r²

• The field points radially away from a positive charge, and radially toward a negative charge.

• It is proportional to Q and inversely proportional to r² — drag the r slider and watch the arrow shrink with distance.

Superposition of Fields

The electric field is a vector. When several point charges act at once, the net field is the vector sum of each one's individual field:

ΣE = E₁ + E₂ + E₃ + ⋯

Each contribution is found from the single-charge formula, then added head-to-tail — never just by adding magnitudes.

Drag Q₁, Q₂, Q₃ around the grid above (or use the ◀▶▲▼ pads) — the field point P stays fixed at the origin so you can see how moving the sources reshapes the net field there.

Electric Field Line Diagrams

Field lines represent the net force direction on a small positive charge at every point in space.

• They originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges.

• They never cross — the field has only one direction at any point.

• The number of lines leaving/entering a charge is proportional to its magnitude.

• Line density (lines per unit area) shows field strength — closer lines mean a stronger field.

Try setting Q₂ to 0 for a single charge, matching signs for two like charges, or opposite signs for a dipole.

Parallel Plates Between two oppositely charged plates, field lines run straight and parallel from the + plate to the − plate — the field is uniform in the middle, with some bowing (fringing) near the edges.
